Start with the idea: what happens during a fault?

A fault is an unintended electrical connection, such as a phase touching ground or two phases touching each other. The system's voltage drives current through the path to the fault. Because the equivalent impedance may be low, fault current can be much larger than normal load current.

Why fault type matters

  • Three-phase fault: all three phases are involved; a balanced positive-sequence model is often sufficient.
  • Single line-to-ground fault: one phase connects to ground; zero-, positive-, and negative-sequence networks are involved.
  • Line-to-line fault: two phases contact; positive- and negative-sequence networks are involved.
  • Double line-to-ground fault: two phases connect to ground; all three sequence networks can be involved.

Quick check: Why does lower fault-path impedance usually mean larger fault current?
Reveal answer

For a fixed voltage, Ohm's law gives current as voltage divided by impedance magnitude. Reducing impedance increases current.

Worked example: Balanced three-phase fault current

Problem statement: Estimate the initial balanced three-phase fault current for a 13.8 kV system when the total Thevenin-plus-fault impedance magnitude is 1.5 Ω.

Given: VLL = 13.8 kV, |Ztotal| = 1.5 Ω.

Find: the approximate line fault current.

  1. Convert line voltage to volts: VLL = 13,800 V.
  2. For the simplified balanced three-phase magnitude model, Ifault ≈ VLL√3 |Ztotal|.
  3. Substitute: Ifault ≈ 13,800√3 × 1.5 ≈ 5,311 A = 5.31 kA.

Final answer: approximate fault current = 5.31 kA.

Interpretation: The estimate increases as equivalent impedance decreases. This is a magnitude-only teaching model, not a complete short-circuit study.

Scope, assumptions & limitations

This is a simplified magnitude-only balanced-fault estimate. Full fault studies use complex sequence impedances, prefault voltage, grounding, and fault type.
